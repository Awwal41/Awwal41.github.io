Models for matter, and the software that makes them usable.

The challenge in materials science is no longer just predicting how atoms behave. It is making those predictions fast enough to explore more materials, test more ideas, and accelerate discovery. That is the problem I work on as a PhD candidate in the Lindsey Lab and a MICDE Fellow.

I develop physics-informed machine-learning models and scientific software that make atomistic simulations more efficient without losing sight of the underlying physics. My focus extends beyond the models themselves to the software that makes them usable, from running simulations on a laptop to scaling them across HPC clusters through existing SLURM workflows.

I also build computational tools and backend services that connect these models to broader research applications. Through collaborations spanning University of Michigan research groups and national-laboratory researchers, including coauthors at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory on our transfer-learning work, I aim to turn advances in scientific machine learning into tools researchers can actually use.

About

Wells lose production when liquid loads the tubing or sand comes up with the flow. From the University of Lagos through later collaborations, my papers use field data and models to see liquid loading, sand, and hydrate temperature early enough to act, and to weigh oil recovery together with carbon storage. Those losses are how domestic wells go offline, and the recovery work keeps carbon storage inside that same field decision. A related study improves biodiesel yield from waste fat by pairing experiment with computation.

At Michigan the bottleneck moved from the well to the simulation: the physics is trustworthy, and the setup is slow. I build public agents and research codes, and private tools for documents and for running models on a laptop, a cluster, or a phone. Private work is described at the level of the contribution, and the repository stays closed.

How I take a problem apart

The same four moves show up in the papers, the agents, and the services. The projects further down are where they were tested.

  1. Keep what must stay true

    I start from the constraint that cannot move. In the research that constraint is the physics. In a document system it is the source. In a protected environment it is the boundary. I spend the effort on the cost: a layered model, a reused fit, a model that runs locally. What remains is a result someone can still trust.

  2. Run it here, then on the cluster

    The first version has to run on the machine in front of you. EZRA, MaterialSim, and the retrieval services all start there, and that same path is what moves onto SLURM, Docker, or Kubernetes. A person can try the tool before they request an allocation or a deployment.

  3. Hide the hard part behind a plain interface

    The person who built the model should not be required for every later use. I wrap the hard part in a sentence, an API, or a calculator an existing simulator can call. MaterialSim, the ChIMES handoff, and the services behind the private tools all do this. More people can use the system, and the internals can change while the interface stays put.

  4. Leave the evidence on the result

    A result that cannot be checked will not be used for a real decision. Retrieval points at the passage. A Bayesian net carries its uncertainty. A review stack inspects untrusted text before it crosses into a protected system. The reader can see why the answer deserves weight.

Where that work sits

Scientific software on HPC systems, applied AI, and the backend that makes either of them usable by someone else.

HPC Scientific software

From a desk model to a cluster job

A potential, a fitter, and a batch system fail when each one is a separate dialect. I work across that stack: machine-learned interatomic potentials, active learning and least-squares fitting in ChIMES, molecular dynamics through MaterialSim, and SLURM staging in EZRA. A model developed at a desk at Michigan can be the job that runs on the SLURM cluster a lab already has, with the physics still the thing being computed.

AI Models and agents

AI that still answers to the science

A model that loses the question it was built to answer is a dead end. I build agents that run simulations from ordinary language, local and on-device language models, retrieval that cites the page, and the interatomic models themselves, including TurboChIMES and hierarchical transfer learning. The AI serves a decision, and the physics or the source stays visible beside the answer.

Backend Services

A stable interface in front of a moving model

A model still needs ingestion, an API, and a client that does not depend on the model’s private shape. I have built retrieval services packaged for Docker, Kubernetes, and cloud accounts, desktop and web clients in front of them, and private product services in TypeScript. Callers get a contract they can build on. The model and the data can change behind it.

Day to day that is Python and TypeScript, REST services, Docker and Kubernetes, SLURM, molecular dynamics, and machine-learned interatomic potentials.

2026 Research
TurboChIMES A designer can try more chemistries before a long campaign.

Accurate atomistic models often cost too much to use while a material is still being designed. TurboChIMES, my first-author preprint, stacks machine-learned interatomic models so the simulation stays faithful and becomes fast enough to search with. This is the story the audience chose as People’s Choice at Michigan’s Three Minute Thesis.

2026 Research
Hierarchical transfer learning Labs can reach a usable potential with less data and less compute.

A new chemistry usually means fitting an interatomic model all over again, and that fit is the slow, expensive part. With Rebecca Lindsey and colleagues at Lawrence Livermore, I am second author on a paper in npj Computational Materials that carries what a model has already learned into the next system.

2026 Research
Alchemical compression Materials design can reach a wider set of elements.

Every added element multiplies the training burden of a machine-learned potential, so coverage stays narrow. With T. Sundberg and Rebecca Lindsey I am compressing that elemental space so one potential can span more of the periodic table. The direction is public as a 2026 AIChE contribution.

2025 Software
MaterialSim A researcher gets a real local run and an interpretation in the same conversation.

A molecular-dynamics study still begins as a specialist’s input deck. MaterialSim is a public agent that turns one sentence into the simulation, computes properties such as structure and transport, and explains what came back. The engine runs locally.

Source

2026 Software Private
EZRA A researcher can work locally the same day and send that same job to a cluster.

Open language models are free, and getting one installed, pointed at your papers, and onto a cluster is still its own project. EZRA downloads a model, runs an agent on the machine in front of you, prepares documents for that model, and stages the same job on SLURM.

2025 Research
Droplet-film model for liquid loading See liquid loading in a gas well early enough to act before a shutdown.

When gas in a well slows down, liquid falls back, pressure rises, and production can stop. I built a physics-informed model of the droplet film behind that failure, with public documentation, extending the liquid-loading study I published with Kingsley Abhulimen.

2025 Research
Sand production Operators get a clearer view of which wells need attention.

Sand produced with oil or gas wears out equipment and cuts a well’s output, and the warning often arrives after the damage. My first-author paper in Petroleum Science and Engineering uses field data to predict and manage that sand, on top of public notebooks for reservoir rock typing.

Paper Rock typing

2025 Product Private
Edna The archive stays searchable, and every answer stays attached to its source.

A question about a long technical archive is only useful when the answer can be traced to a page. Edna is a private assistant with a desktop app and a web client: you choose the folders, ask, and the reply points at the passage it used. It can run on one computer or as a service you operate.

2023– Software
ChIMES toolchain The model can leave the notebook and run inside a simulator a lab already trusts.

A machine-learned interatomic model helps only after it can be fit, checked, and called from the simulator a lab already trusts. I work in the public ChIMES stack that does the active learning, the least-squares fit, and the handoff into outside codes.
